# Cyber Kill Chain

## Introduction

The **Cyber Kill Chain** is a cybersecurity framework introduced by **Lockheed Martin in 2011**. It was inspired by military kill-chain concepts and is used to understand the different stages of a cyber attack.

The framework divides an attack into **seven stages**. Understanding these stages helps defenders identify where an attack can be detected, disrupted, or stopped.

### Seven Stages

<pre>
Reconnaissance
      ↓
Weaponisation
      ↓
Delivery
      ↓
Exploitation
      ↓
Installation
      ↓
Command & Control
      ↓
Actions on Objectives
</pre>

---

## 1. Reconnaissance

The attacker gathers information about the target before attempting an attack.

Examples:

* Identifying systems and services
* Discovering domains and IP addresses
* Gathering information about employees or technologies
* Identifying potential vulnerabilities

**Goal:** Understand the target and identify potential attack opportunities.

---

## 2. Weaponisation

The attacker prepares a **weaponised payload** based on information gathered during reconnaissance.

This may involve:

* Creating a malicious payload.
* Modifying an existing payload.
* Combining an exploit with a payload.
* Preparing a document or other delivery mechanism.

**Goal:** Prepare something capable of exploiting the identified target.

---

## 3. Delivery

The attacker transmits or delivers the weaponised payload to the target.

Possible delivery methods include:

* Phishing emails
* Malicious documents
* Exploited websites
* Removable media
* Other communication channels

**Goal:** Get the weaponised content to the target system or user.

---

## 4. Exploitation

The delivered payload exploits a vulnerability or weakness in the target.

<pre>
Payload
   ↓
Vulnerability
   ↓
Exploitation
   ↓
Initial Compromise
</pre>

**Goal:** Gain execution or access through the targeted weakness.

---

## 5. Installation

After successful exploitation, the attacker may install malware, a backdoor, or another mechanism to maintain **persistence** within the environment.

**Goal:** Maintain access beyond the initial compromise.

---

## 6. Command & Control (C2)

The compromised system establishes communication with infrastructure controlled by the attacker.

Through this communication, an attacker may:

* Send commands.
* Receive information.
* Control the compromised system.
* Deliver additional tools or payloads.

**Goal:** Maintain and control the compromised system.

---

## 7. Actions on Objectives

This is the stage where the attacker performs the intended objective of the attack.

Possible objectives include:

* Stealing sensitive data.
* Disrupting systems.
* Destroying or modifying information.
* Moving to additional systems.
* Establishing further access.

**Goal:** Achieve the attacker's ultimate objective.

---

## Cyber Kill Chain Model

<pre>
1. Reconnaissance
         ↓
   Find the target

2. Weaponisation
         ↓
   Prepare the attack

3.   Delivery
         ↓
   Deliver the payload

4. Exploitation
         ↓
   Exploit a weakness

5. Installation
         ↓
   Establish persistence

6. Command & Control
         ↓
   Control the compromised system

7. Actions on Objectives
         ↓
   Achieve the intended goal
</pre>
  
### Key Takeaway

The Cyber Kill Chain provides a structured way to understand an attack from **initial information gathering to the attacker's final objective**.

The defensive value comes from recognising that an attack is a chain of stages. **Breaking any stage can disrupt or prevent the attack from progressing further.**

---

## Reconnaissance

**Reconnaissance** is the first stage of the Cyber Kill Chain. It involves gathering information about a target to identify potential weaknesses, attack surfaces, and entry points.

The concept originates from military reconnaissance, where information is collected about an opposing target before an operation.

<pre>
Reconnaissance
      ↓
Gather Information
      ↓
Identify Attack Surface
      ↓
Find Potential Entry Points
</pre>

Reconnaissance is generally divided into **passive** and **active** reconnaissance.

---

## Passive Reconnaissance

**Passive reconnaissance** involves gathering information without directly interacting with the target's systems in a way that would typically generate noticeable activity.

Common sources include **OSINT (Open-Source Intelligence)**.

### Examples

#### WHOIS

WHOIS information can provide details such as:

* Domain registration information
* Registration dates
* Domain ownership information
* Contact details

Privacy services can hide some of this information.

#### DNS Reconnaissance

DNS information can reveal:

* DNS servers
* Domain records
* Public IP addresses
* Other publicly exposed infrastructure

#### Website Reconnaissance

Public websites can be examined through techniques such as:

* Crawling
* Scraping
* Analysing publicly available content

#### Social Media Reconnaissance

Public social media information may reveal:

* Employees
* Technologies used
* Organisational structure
* Publicly disclosed information

#### Google Dorking

Search engines can be used with specialised search operators to locate publicly indexed information that may not be easily visible through normal searches.

---

## Active Reconnaissance

**Active reconnaissance** involves directly interacting with the target or its infrastructure. Because the target may observe this activity, it is generally more detectable than passive reconnaissance.

### Examples

#### Port Scanning

Port scanning can identify:

* Live hosts
* Open ports
* Running services
* Potential attack surface

<pre>
Target
  ↓
Port Scan
  ↓
Open Ports
  ↓
Running Services
</pre>

#### Vulnerability Scanning

Vulnerability scanners can interact with exposed services to identify potential weaknesses.

This produces more direct interaction with the target and may generate logs or alerts.

#### Social Engineering

An attacker may interact with employees or personnel to gather information that could help identify potential attack paths.

#### Physical Reconnaissance

Physical reconnaissance involves observing the target's physical environment.

Examples include:

* Identifying physical entry points.
* Observing security controls.
* Studying access procedures.
* Observing personnel behaviour.

---

## Passive vs Active Reconnaissance

| Factor          | Passive Reconnaissance                                                 | Active Reconnaissance                                                            |
| --------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Interaction** | Minimal or no direct interaction with target systems.                  | Direct interaction with target systems or personnel.                             |
| **Visibility**  | Generally less detectable.                                             | More likely to generate logs or alerts.                                          |
| **Examples**    | WHOIS, DNS information, social media, search engines, public websites. | Port scanning, vulnerability scanning, social engineering, physical observation. |
| **Goal**        | Gather publicly available information.                                 | Directly identify systems, services, weaknesses, or behaviours.                  |

---

## Countermeasures Against Reconnaissance

Organisations can reduce reconnaissance opportunities by limiting unnecessary information exposure and monitoring for suspicious activity.

### 1. Minimise Public Information

Limit unnecessary information exposed through:

* Websites
* Social media
* DNS records
* Public documentation

Only information required for legitimate business purposes should be publicly available.

### 2. Protect WHOIS Information

Where appropriate, organisations can use domain privacy services to reduce exposure of personal contact information in registration records.

### 3. Monitor Network Traffic

Security teams should monitor network traffic for suspicious reconnaissance activity, including:

* Port scans
* Unusual connection patterns
* Repeated requests to multiple services

### 4. Monitor Logs

Service and network logs can help identify reconnaissance attempts and unusual activity against exposed infrastructure.

<pre>
Reconnaissance Activity
        ↓
Network / Service Logs
        ↓
  Detection
        ↓
  Investigation
        ↓
    Response
</pre>

### Key Takeaways

* **Reconnaissance is the first Cyber Kill Chain stage.**
* **Passive reconnaissance** gathers information with minimal direct interaction.
* **Active reconnaissance** directly interacts with systems, services, or personnel.
* Reconnaissance helps identify the target's **attack surface and potential entry points**.
* Defenders can reduce reconnaissance opportunities by **minimising public information and monitoring network and service activity**.

---

## Weaponisation

**Weaponisation** is the stage where an attacker uses information gathered during reconnaissance to prepare a malicious payload designed to exploit the target.

The attacker may:

* Use an existing exploit.
* Modify an existing exploit.
* Develop a custom exploit or payload.
* Use frameworks and exploit toolkits to simplify payload creation.
* Apply **obfuscation or encryption** to make detection more difficult.
* Hide malicious content inside files that appear legitimate, such as documents.

### Weaponisation Flow

```text
Reconnaissance
      ↓
Identify Vulnerability
      ↓
Select / Create Exploit
      ↓
Build Malicious Payload
      ↓
Obfuscate / Hide Payload
      ↓
Ready for Delivery
```

The important distinction is that **weaponisation prepares the attack**, while the actual delivery happens in the next stage.

---

## What Can Be Weaponised ?

Attackers can prepare malicious payloads in different forms depending on the target and attack method.

Examples include:

* Email attachments
* Malicious Office documents
* Executable files
* PDF or other document files
* USB devices
* Web-hosted payloads
* Exploits targeting vulnerable network services

The chosen method depends on the weaknesses identified during reconnaissance.

---

## Exploit Kits

An **exploit kit** is a collection or framework containing multiple exploits that can be used against different vulnerabilities.

They can help attackers:

* Select an exploit matching a known vulnerability.
* Package exploit code into a payload.
* Automate parts of the exploitation process.
* Prepare payloads for different delivery methods.

From a defensive perspective, exploit kits increase the importance of **vulnerability management and endpoint protection**.

---

## Malicious Office Documents

A common weaponisation technique involves creating Office documents containing **malicious macros**.

A macro is a saved set of instructions that can automatically execute when a document is opened, depending on the application's security settings.

<pre>
Malicious Office Document
          ↓
       User Opens
          ↓
     Macro Executes
          ↓
   Malicious Actions
</pre>

Modern security controls can restrict macros, especially when documents originate from untrusted sources.

---

## Obfuscation and Encryption

Attackers may attempt to make malicious payloads harder for security tools to identify.

### Obfuscation

**Obfuscation** changes the appearance or structure of malicious code without changing its intended behavior.

### Encryption

**Encryption** can conceal the contents of a payload until it is decrypted.

These techniques are commonly used as **defence-evasion mechanisms**.

> Obfuscation or encryption does not make malware inherently safe, it is primarily intended to make analysis or detection more difficult.

---

## Preparing for Delivery

Once the malicious payload has been created, the attacker needs a way to deliver it to the target.

Possible delivery methods include:

<pre>
Weaponised Payload
       │
       ├── Phishing Email
       ├── Malicious Website
       ├── USB Device
       ├── Malicious Document
       └── Vulnerable Network Service
</pre>

The exact delivery method depends on the attacker's objective and the target's attack surface.

---

## Countermeasures Against Weaponisation

Defenders can reduce the effectiveness of weaponisation by limiting the attacker's ability to create, deliver, or execute malicious payloads.

### 1. Security Awareness Training

Users should be trained to recognize suspicious:

* Email attachments
* Links
* Unexpected documents
* Requests for passwords or sensitive information
* Encrypted archives accompanied by passwords

Users should also understand how to verify the legitimacy of suspicious emails rather than immediately opening attachments.

### 2. Disable Unnecessary Features

Reduce the attack surface by disabling features that are not required.

Examples:

* Disable unnecessary Office macros.
* Allow macros only from trusted or signed sources where appropriate.
* Remove unnecessary browser plugins.
* Uninstall unused software.
* Disable unnecessary services and features.

### 3. Apply Security Policies

Organizations can enforce security controls centrally through mechanisms such as **Windows Group Policy**.

For example:

<pre>
Group Policy
     ↓
Restrict Risky Features
     ↓
Reduce Attack Surface
     ↓
Limit Payload Execution
</pre>

### 4. Keep Software Updated

Regular patching reduces the number of known vulnerabilities that attackers can use when preparing their payloads.

---

## Key Takeaways

* **Weaponisation** converts reconnaissance findings into a malicious payload.
* Attackers can use existing exploits, modified exploits, or custom payloads.
* Exploit kits can simplify the process of preparing attacks.
* Payloads may be hidden inside seemingly legitimate files or delivered through other channels.
* **Obfuscation and encryption** can be used to make malicious content harder to detect or analyze.
* Malicious Office macros are one example of how documents can be weaponised.
* Security awareness, patching, attack-surface reduction, and restrictive policies can reduce the impact of weaponisation.
* The final goal of this stage is a **weaponised payload ready for delivery**.

---

## Delivery

**Delivery** is the stage where the attacker transmits the weaponised payload to the target environment.

The delivery method is usually selected based on information gathered during **reconnaissance**. The attacker attempts to choose a method that increases the probability that the target will receive and interact with the payload.

### Delivery Flow

<pre>
Weaponised Payload
       ↓
Select Delivery Method
       ↓
Transmit to Target
       ↓
Target Receives Payload
       ↓
Ready for Exploitation
</pre>

---

## Common Delivery Methods

### 1. Phishing

Attackers send deceptive emails containing:

* Malicious attachments
* Links to malicious downloads
* Instructions designed to convince the recipient to execute a file

File names can also be manipulated to appear legitimate.

Example:

<pre>
invoice.pdf.exe
</pre>

may appear more trustworthy at a glance than:

<pre>
program.exe
</pre>

This relies on the user overlooking the actual file extension.

### 2. Spear Phishing

**Spear phishing** is a targeted form of phishing where the attacker customizes the message for a specific person or organization.

The attacker may impersonate:

* A manager
* A colleague
* A known organization
* A trusted service

The goal is to make the communication appear legitimate and increase the likelihood that the victim will interact with it.

<pre>
Generic Phishing
      ↓
Mass / Broad Targeting

Spear Phishing
      ↓
Specific Target
      ↓
Personalized Message
</pre>

### 3. Malicious Web Links

Attackers may host malicious content on websites and convince users to visit them.

Techniques may include:

* Deceptive domains
* URL shortening
* Lookalike websites
* Malicious download pages

The objective is to make the malicious destination appear trustworthy enough for the target to visit or download content.

### 4. File-Sharing Platforms

Attackers may upload malicious files to legitimate file-sharing services and distribute links to those files.

This can abuse the trust users already have in familiar file-sharing platforms.

### 5. Malvertising

**Malvertising** is the use of malicious advertisements to redirect users toward malicious content.

<pre>
Legitimate Website
       ↓
Malicious Advertisement
       ↓
Redirect
       ↓
Malicious Website / Payload
</pre>

The victim may never intentionally search for the malicious website.

### 6. SMS Phishing (Smishing)

**Smishing** is phishing performed through SMS or text messages.

A message may contain:

* A malicious link
* A fake security notification
* A request to download an application
* A request for credentials or sensitive information

The attacker attempts to use the urgency and trust associated with mobile messages to encourage interaction.

### 7. Social Engineering

Social engineering uses **human interaction and manipulation** rather than relying entirely on technical vulnerabilities.

For example, an attacker may convince a user to:

1. Download a program.
2. Open a malicious document.
3. Visit a malicious website.
4. Provide information that enables further attack activity.

The technical payload may be effective only if the attacker successfully convinces the target to interact with it.

### 8. Physical Delivery

Payloads can also be delivered through physical media.

Examples include:

* USB drives
* DVDs
* Other removable storage devices

For example:

<pre>
Malicious USB
     ↓
Placed in Accessible Location
     ↓
User Connects USB
     ↓
Malicious File Executed
</pre>

Physical delivery may rely heavily on **social engineering and context** to convince someone to use the device.

## Delivery Methods - Quick Comparison

| Method             | Primary Channel       | Typical Target          |
| ------------------ | --------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Phishing           | Email                 | Users                   |
| Spear Phishing     | Targeted Email        | Specific individuals    |
| Malicious Links    | Web                   | Web users               |
| File Sharing       | Online services       | Users / organizations   |
| Malvertising       | Advertisements        | Website visitors        |
| Smishing           | SMS                   | Mobile users            |
| Social Engineering | Human interaction     | Individuals / employees |
| Physical Delivery  | USB / removable media | Organizations / users   |

---

## Countermeasures Against Delivery

Defenders need multiple layers of protection because attackers can use different delivery channels.

### 1. Security Awareness Training

Users should be trained to identify:

* Phishing emails
* Suspicious attachments
* Malicious links
* Social engineering attempts
* Unexpected downloads
* Suspicious SMS messages

Security awareness should be an ongoing process rather than a one-time activity.

### 2. Email Filtering

Email security controls can inspect incoming messages for indicators such as:

* Suspicious attachments
* Malicious URLs
* Spoofed senders
* Known malicious content

This can prevent many payloads from reaching users.

### 3. Web Filtering

Web filtering can restrict access to known malicious or suspicious websites and help prevent users from downloading harmful content.

### 4. Web Application Firewalls

A **Web Application Firewall (WAF)** can inspect and filter HTTP/HTTPS traffic directed toward web applications.

It can help protect against malicious requests and certain types of web-based attacks.

> A WAF is primarily an application-layer defence, it is not a universal solution for every payload delivery technique.

### 5. Network Monitoring

Monitoring network traffic can help identify suspicious activity such as:

* Connections to known malicious destinations
* Unusual downloads
* Suspicious outbound traffic
* Unexpected communication patterns

### 6. Patch Management

Keeping operating systems, applications, browsers, and network services updated reduces the number of known vulnerabilities available to attackers.


## Breaking the Kill Chain

Delivery is a critical opportunity for defenders to stop the attack before the payload reaches the exploitation stage.

<pre>
Weaponisation
      ↓
 DELIVERY
      ↓
Exploitation
</pre>

If the malicious payload is blocked during delivery, later stages of the attack may never occur.

---

## Key Takeaways

* **Delivery** is the stage where the weaponised payload is transmitted to the target.
* Attackers can use email, web links, SMS, file-sharing services, advertisements, social engineering, or physical media.
* **Spear phishing** is more targeted and personalized than generic phishing.
* Delivery attacks often combine **technical methods with social engineering**.
* Security awareness, email/web filtering, network monitoring, WAFs, and patch management can help disrupt delivery.
* Blocking the payload during delivery can prevent the attack from progressing to **Exploitation**.

---

## Exploitation

**Exploitation** is the stage where the attacker actively takes advantage of a weakness in the target system to gain access, execute code, bypass security controls, or perform another unauthorized action.

The weakness being exploited could be:

* A software vulnerability
* A weak or compromised password
* A system misconfiguration
* A vulnerable network service
* A web application vulnerability

### Exploitation Flow

<pre>
Delivered Payload
       ↓
Identify Vulnerability / Weakness
       ↓
Exploit Weakness
       ↓
Gain Access / Execute Action
       ↓
Continue Attack
</pre>

---

## Common Exploitation Techniques

### 1. Weak or Default Passwords

Password-based authentication can be attacked when users or systems use:

* Default credentials
* Weak passwords
* Reused passwords
* Easily guessable passwords

An attacker may also obtain credentials through phishing or other credential-theft techniques.

<pre>
Weak Credentials
      ↓
Successful Authentication
      ↓
Unauthorized Access
</pre>

### 2. Software Vulnerabilities

Software vulnerabilities can exist in:

* Client applications
* Operating systems
* Network services
* Web applications
* Server software

Once a vulnerability becomes known, attackers may develop exploit code that takes advantage of it.

Regular vulnerability discovery means organizations must continuously monitor and patch their systems.

### 3. Zero-Day Exploits

A **zero-day vulnerability** is a previously unknown vulnerability for which there is no effective vendor patch or established defence at the time it is exploited.

An exploit targeting such a vulnerability is commonly called a **zero-day exploit**.

<pre>
Unknown Vulnerability
        ↓
Attacker Discovers / Obtains Knowledge
        ↓
Exploit Developed
        ↓
Attack Before Effective Patch
</pre>

Zero-day exploitation can be particularly difficult to defend against because traditional patch-based protection may not yet be available.

### 4. Web Application Vulnerabilities

Attackers can exploit vulnerabilities in web applications to perform unauthorized actions.

Examples include:

* **SQL Injection (SQLi)** - manipulating database queries.
* **Cross-Site Scripting (XSS)** - injecting malicious client-side scripts.
* **Authentication vulnerabilities** - bypassing or abusing authentication mechanisms.
* **Access control vulnerabilities** - accessing resources without proper authorization.

### 5. Buffer Overflow

A **buffer overflow** occurs when a program writes more data into a memory buffer than it was designed to hold.

Depending on the vulnerability and system protections, this may allow an attacker to:

* Crash the application.
* Corrupt memory.
* Alter program execution.
* Potentially execute attacker-controlled code.

---

## Exploitation Without Valid Credentials

Not every exploitation technique requires legitimate login credentials.

For example :

<pre>
Internet-Facing Service
        ↓
Vulnerable Software
        ↓
    Exploit
        ↓
Unauthorized Access
</pre>

This is why exposed and vulnerable services are an important part of an organization's attack surface.

---

## Countermeasures Against Exploitation

### 1. Strong Authentication

Organizations should enforce strong password requirements and use **Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)** where appropriate.

MFA adds an additional authentication factor, making stolen passwords alone insufficient in many scenarios.

<pre>
Username + Password
        +
Additional Factor
        ↓
Stronger Authentication
</pre>

> MFA reduces the risk from stolen credentials, but it does not protect against every type of exploitation.

### 2. Patch Management

Regularly applying security updates helps remove known vulnerabilities from:

* Operating systems
* Servers
* Applications
* Network services
* Client systems

A strong patch-management process reduces the window during which known vulnerabilities can be exploited.

### 3. Vulnerability Scanning

Regular vulnerability scanning helps identify:

* Missing security patches
* Vulnerable software versions
* Misconfigurations
* Exposed services
* Other known weaknesses

<pre>
Scan
 ↓
Identify Vulnerability
 ↓
Prioritize
 ↓
Patch / Remediate
 ↓
Rescan
</pre>

### 4. Intrusion Prevention Systems (IPS)

An **Intrusion Prevention System (IPS)** monitors network traffic and can actively block traffic matching known malicious or exploit patterns.

This can help prevent exploitation attempts from reaching vulnerable systems.

### 5. Web Application Firewall (WAF)

A **Web Application Firewall (WAF)** filters HTTP/HTTPS traffic directed at web applications.

Depending on its configuration and capabilities, a WAF can help detect or block attacks such as:

* SQL Injection
* Cross-Site Scripting (XSS)
* Cross-Site Request Forgery (CSRF)

A WAF should be treated as an additional security layer rather than a replacement for secure application development and patching.

---

## Breaking the Kill Chain

Exploitation provides another opportunity for defenders to interrupt the attack.

<pre>
Delivery
   ↓
EXPLOITATION  ← Detect / Block
   ↓
Installation
   ↓
Command & Control
</pre>

Preventing the vulnerability from being exploited can stop the attacker before persistence or command-and-control activity is established.

---

## Key Takeaways

* **Exploitation** is where an attacker takes advantage of a weakness in the target.
* Weak passwords, misconfigurations, and software vulnerabilities can all enable exploitation.
* Vulnerabilities may exist in both client systems and network services.
* **Zero-day exploits** target previously unknown vulnerabilities before effective vendor fixes are available.
* Common vulnerability classes include **SQL injection, XSS, and buffer overflows**.
* Strong authentication, MFA, patch management, vulnerability scanning, IPS, and WAFs can reduce exploitation risk.
* Exploitation can occur even without valid credentials when an exposed service contains a remotely exploitable vulnerability.

---

## Installation

**Installation** is the stage where the attacker establishes **persistence** on a compromised system.

After successful exploitation, the attacker wants to ensure they can regain access later without repeating the original exploitation process.

The key concept in this stage is:

> **Persistence - maintaining access after the initial compromise.**

### Installation Flow

<pre>
Exploitation
     ↓
Compromise System
     ↓
Establish Persistence
     ↓
Maintain Access
     ↓
Ready for Command & Control
</pre>

---

## Common Persistence Techniques

### 1. Scheduled Tasks

Attackers may create scheduled tasks that automatically execute malicious programs at a specific time or event.

On Windows, this can involve **Scheduled Tasks**.

<pre>
Scheduled Task
      ↓
Trigger Condition
      ↓
Malicious Program Executes
      ↓
Access Restored
</pre>

### 2. Cron Jobs

Linux systems can use **cron jobs** to execute commands or scripts automatically according to a schedule.

An attacker who obtains sufficient privileges may abuse this functionality to maintain persistence.

### 3. Startup Scripts and Configuration Files

Attackers may modify startup-related scripts or configuration files so that malicious programs execute automatically when the system or a particular service starts.

### 4. Malicious Services and Daemons

Attackers may create or modify services to automatically execute malicious software.

Examples include:

* **Windows Services**
* **Linux daemons**

Services are useful for persistence because they can automatically start when the operating system boots or when specific conditions occur.

---

## Malware and Backdoors

Attackers may install:

* Malware
* Backdoors
* Rootkits
* Other persistence mechanisms

A **backdoor** provides an alternative method of accessing a compromised system.

A **rootkit** is designed to hide malicious activity or maintain privileged access while attempting to evade detection.

---

## Living-off-the-Land Binaries (LOLBins)

Attackers do not always need to introduce new tools onto a compromised system.

They may abuse legitimate operating-system utilities that are already installed.

These are commonly referred to as **Living-off-the-Land Binaries (LOLBins)**.

<pre>
Legitimate System Utility
          ↓
   Abused by Attacker
          ↓
Execute Malicious Activity
</pre>

This technique can make detection more difficult because the activity may involve legitimate system components rather than obviously malicious executables.

---

## Web Shells

When a web application is compromised, an attacker may deploy a **web shell**.

A web shell is a malicious script hosted on a web server that allows an attacker to execute commands through web requests.

Conceptually:

<pre>
Compromised Web Application
          ↓
      Web Shell
          ↓
   Web Request
          ↓
Operating System Command
</pre>

The web shell must use a programming language supported by the target server.

For example, a compromised server supporting a particular server-side scripting language may execute a web shell written in that language.

Attackers may communicate with a web shell over standard web protocols such as **HTTPS**, which can make the traffic blend with normal web traffic.

---

## Countermeasures Against Installation

### 1. Monitor Processes and Services

Security teams should monitor:

* New processes
* New services
* Unusual parent-child process relationships
* Unexpected executable files
* Suspicious process activity

Understanding the **context and relationship between processes** is important because legitimate processes can also be abused.

### 2. Endpoint Detection and Response (EDR)

**EDR** solutions continuously monitor endpoints for suspicious activity.

Examples of useful detection signals include:

* Unexpected process creation
* Modification of sensitive files
* New services
* Changes to startup locations
* Suspicious network connections
* Unusual command execution

<pre>
Endpoint Activity
       ↓
EDR Monitoring
       ↓
Detect Suspicious Behaviour
       ↓
Investigate / Respond
</pre>

### 3. System Auditing

Regular system audits can identify unauthorized changes such as:

* Newly created user accounts
* Unexpected services
* Modified configuration files
* Changed startup mechanisms
* Unauthorized software

Organizations can compare systems against a known **secure baseline** to identify deviations.

### 4. Configuration Management

Configuration-management tools can help organizations:

* Maintain standardized configurations.
* Detect unauthorized changes.
* Automatically restore approved configurations.
* Maintain system consistency.

<pre>
Secure Baseline
      ↓
Compare Current State
      ↓
Detect Unauthorized Change
      ↓
Remediate
</pre>

### 5. Application Allowlisting

**Application allowlisting** restricts software execution to applications that have been explicitly approved.

<pre>
Application Execution Request
          ↓
Is Application Approved?
       ↙       ↘
     YES        NO
      ↓          ↓
   Execute      Block
</pre>

This can reduce the ability of attackers to execute unauthorized malware or tools.

---

## Breaking the Kill Chain

Installation is an important defensive checkpoint because successful persistence allows an attacker to continue operating even after the original vulnerability is no longer available.

<pre>
Exploitation
     ↓
INSTALLATION  ← Detect / Remove Persistence
     ↓
Command & Control
     ↓
Actions on Objectives


If persistence mechanisms are identified and removed, the attacker may need to repeat the initial access process.
</pre>

## Key Takeaways

* **Installation** establishes persistence after successful exploitation.
* Persistence allows attackers to regain access without repeating the original exploitation.
* Common persistence mechanisms include scheduled tasks, cron jobs, startup scripts, services, malware, and backdoors.
* **LOLBins** allow attackers to abuse legitimate system utilities instead of relying exclusively on new malicious software.
* **Web shells** can provide command execution through a compromised web server.
* EDR, system auditing, secure baselines, configuration management, and application allowlisting can help detect or prevent persistence.
* Detecting and removing persistence can break the attack chain before **Command & Control** is established.

---

## Command and Control (C2)

**Command and Control (C2)** is the stage where an attacker establishes communication between compromised systems and attacker-controlled infrastructure.

After persistence has been established, the attacker needs a reliable way to:

* Send commands to compromised systems.
* Receive responses or collected information.
* Maintain remote access.
* Coordinate activity across multiple compromised systems.
* Prepare for the final objectives of the attack.

### C2 Flow

<pre>
Attacker Infrastructure
          ↕
   C2 Communication
          ↕
   Compromised System
</pre>

The attacker uses the C2 channel to send commands to compromised systems and receive responses or collected data in return.

The attacker attempts to make this communication **reliable, covert, and difficult to disrupt**.

---

## C2 Infrastructure

An attacker may build C2 infrastructure using :

* IP addresses
* Domain names
* Cloud services
* Compromised systems
* Other internet-accessible services

Attackers may also use techniques intended to make the infrastructure harder to identify or take down.

Examples include:

* Frequently changing IP addresses.
* Generating many domain names.
* Using legitimate-looking domains.
* Encrypting C2 traffic.
* Hiding communication inside commonly used protocols.

---

## Common C2 Communication Methods

### 1. Common Application-Layer Protocols

Attackers may use protocols that are already common within normal network traffic.

Examples include:

* **HTTP**
* **HTTPS**
* **DNS**
* **SMTP**

Using common protocols can make malicious traffic harder to distinguish from legitimate communication.

<pre>
Malware
   ↓
HTTP / HTTPS / DNS / SMTP
   ↓
Internet
   ↓
C2 Server
</pre>

### 2. Encrypted C2

Attackers may use encrypted communication, such as **HTTPS**, to prevent network monitoring systems from easily inspecting the contents of the traffic.

Encryption can protect the communication content, although metadata and traffic patterns may still provide useful detection signals.

### 3. DNS Tunnelling

**DNS tunnelling** involves encoding information inside DNS queries or responses to create a communication channel.

Conceptually:

<pre>
Compromised System
       ↓
Encoded DNS Request
       ↓
DNS Infrastructure
       ↓
Attacker-Controlled System
</pre>

Because DNS is widely used in normal networks, abnormal DNS-based communication can sometimes blend into legitimate traffic.

### 4. Social Media and Cloud Services

Attackers may abuse legitimate platforms as part of their C2 infrastructure.

Examples can include:

* Social media messaging
* Cloud storage
* Online document services
* Other legitimate web services

The goal is to make malicious communication appear similar to normal traffic involving trusted services.

---

## Resilient C2 Infrastructure

Attackers may design their infrastructure so that blocking one domain or IP address does not completely disrupt communication.

Two techniques discussed in the Cyber Kill Chain are **Domain Generation Algorithms (DGAs)** and **Fast Flux**.

---

## Domain Generation Algorithms (DGA)

A **Domain Generation Algorithm (DGA)** is a technique where malware automatically generates large numbers of domain names according to a predefined algorithm.

For example:

<pre>
Malware
   ↓
Generate Many Possible Domains
   ↓
Try Domains
   ↓
Find Active C2 Domain
   ↓
Establish Communication
</pre>

The attacker may register only a small subset of the generated domains.

If one domain is blocked or taken down, the malware can attempt another generated domain.

### Defensive Challenge

Instead of blocking one known domain, defenders may need to identify the **algorithmic patterns or behavioural characteristics** associated with generated domains.

---

## Fast Flux

**Fast Flux** is a technique where a domain is associated with many IP addresses, with the associated addresses changing frequently.

Compromised systems may also act as intermediary nodes that forward traffic toward the hidden C2 infrastructure.

<pre>
                 ┌── IP 1
                 ├── IP 2
Domain ──────────┼── IP 3
                 ├── IP 4
                 └── IP 5
                      ↓
                   Hidden C2
</pre>

If one IP address is blocked, another can be used.

### DGA vs Fast Flux

| Technique     | Main Idea                                                   |
| ------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| **DGA**       | Generate many possible domain names                         |
| **Fast Flux** | Frequently change the IP addresses associated with a domain |
| **Purpose**   | Make C2 infrastructure harder to block or take down         |

---

## Countermeasures Against C2

C2 detection requires monitoring both **network behaviour** and **communication patterns**.

### 1. Network Monitoring

Firewalls, IDS, and IPS can help detect and block suspicious communication.

Security teams can monitor for:

* Connections to known malicious IP addresses.
* Unusual traffic volumes.
* Unexpected outbound connections.
* Suspicious communication patterns.
* Repeated connections to unusual destinations.

### 2. DNS Monitoring

DNS traffic should be monitored for indicators such as:

* Unusually long DNS queries.
* High volumes of DNS requests.
* Repeated queries to suspicious domains.
* Algorithmically generated domain patterns.
* Unusual DNS request/response behaviour.

<pre>
DNS Traffic
    ↓
Analyse Queries
    ↓
Identify Anomalies
    ↓
Investigate Suspicious Domains
</pre>

### 3. Web Traffic Monitoring

Because HTTP and HTTPS can be used for C2 communication, monitoring web traffic can help identify suspicious connections.

Security controls can also use **content filtering** to restrict access to known malicious or suspicious URLs.

### 4. HTTPS Inspection

Encrypted HTTPS traffic can make content-based inspection difficult.

Where organizational policy, privacy requirements, and technical architecture permit it, **TLS/HTTPS inspection** can allow security controls to inspect encrypted traffic for malicious activity.

However, inspection must be carefully designed because it introduces privacy, performance, and certificate-management considerations.

### 5. Honeypots

A **honeypot** is a deliberately monitored system designed to attract or detect unauthorized activity.

Honeypots can help security teams:

* Detect suspicious connections.
* Observe attacker behaviour.
* Analyze attempted C2 communication.
* Gather indicators of compromise.

<pre>
Suspicious Activity
       ↓
     Honeypot
       ↓
Detect + Observe
       ↓
Analyze Attacker Behaviour
</pre>

---

## Breaking the Kill Chain

C2 provides an important opportunity to detect an attacker after persistence has been established.

<pre>
Installation
     ↓
COMMAND & CONTROL  ← Detect / Disrupt
     ↓
Actions on Objectives
</pre>

Blocking or disrupting C2 communication can prevent an attacker from reliably controlling compromised systems or moving toward their objectives.

---

## Key Takeaways

* **Command and Control (C2)** establishes communication between compromised systems and attacker-controlled infrastructure.
* C2 channels may use common protocols such as **HTTP, HTTPS, DNS, and SMTP**.
* **DNS tunnelling** can hide communication within DNS traffic.
* Attackers may abuse legitimate cloud or social platforms to make communication harder to distinguish from normal activity.
* **DGA** generates many possible domain names, while **Fast Flux** rapidly changes the IP addresses associated with a domain.
* Network monitoring, DNS analysis, web filtering, IDS/IPS, and appropriate HTTPS inspection can help detect C2 activity.
* Honeypots can provide additional visibility into suspicious communication and attacker behaviour.
* Disrupting C2 can prevent compromised systems from receiving commands and can break the attack chain before **Actions on Objectives**.

---

## Actions on Objectives

**Actions on Objectives** is the final stage of the Cyber Kill Chain. After establishing a reliable **Command & Control (C2)** channel, the attacker can execute the original objectives of the attack.

These objectives may include:

* Data theft and exfiltration
* Financial fraud
* Ransomware
* Destruction or corruption of data
* Service disruption
* Lateral movement
* Espionage
* Manipulation of critical systems
* Maintaining long-term access for future attacks

### Attack Flow

<pre>
Command & Control
        ↓
Execute Attack Objective
        ↓
Steal / Modify / Disrupt / Expand
        ↓
Achieve Attacker's Goal
</pre>

---

## Actions on Objectives: Examples

The specific objective depends on the attacker's **motives and the target organisation**.

### 1. Data Exfiltration

Attackers may steal sensitive information from compromised systems.

Examples:

* Customer data
* Credentials
* Financial records
* Intellectual property
* Confidential documents
* Internal communications

**Data exfiltration** is the unauthorised transfer of data from a target environment to an attacker-controlled location.

### 2. Ransomware

An attacker may encrypt important files and demand payment for their recovery.

<pre>
Compromise
    ↓
Access Sensitive Files
    ↓
Encrypt Data
    ↓
Disrupt Operations
    ↓
Demand Ransom
</pre>

The objective is usually financial gain and disruption of normal operations.

### 3. Financial Theft and Fraud

Attackers may use compromised accounts or systems to perform unauthorised financial activities.

Examples:

* Unauthorised wire transfers
* Fraudulent transactions
* Theft of financial information
* Abuse of compromised banking or payment accounts

### 4. Destruction and Disruption

Some attacks are intended primarily to damage systems or interrupt services.

Examples:

* Deleting important files
* Corrupting databases
* Disrupting critical services
* Destroying system configurations
* Making systems unavailable

The objective is to affect the organisation's normal operations.

### 5. Lateral Movement

After compromising one system, an attacker may attempt to access other systems within the network.

<pre>
Compromised System
        ↓
Discover Internal Network
        ↓
Obtain Additional Access
        ↓
Compromise Other Systems
        ↓
Reach Valuable Target
</pre>

This allows an attacker to move from an initial foothold toward more valuable systems and data.

### 6. Espionage and Sensitive Information Theft

In espionage attacks, the attacker may remain hidden for a long period while collecting sensitive information.

Targets may include:

* Intellectual property
* Research and development data
* Government information
* Business strategies
* Confidential documents

The attacker may prioritise stealth rather than immediate disruption.

> Espionage means secretly collecting confidential or sensitive information from another person, organisation, or government, usually to gain an advantage.

> In cybersecurity:

> Cyber espionage = secretly stealing sensitive information from a target without being detected.

### 7. Industrial Control System (ICS) Manipulation

Attackers targeting industrial environments may attempt to manipulate **Industrial Control Systems (ICS)** or other operational technology.

Potential objectives include:

* Changing system parameters
* Disrupting industrial processes
* Manipulating equipment
* Affecting physical operations

Such attacks can have consequences beyond traditional IT systems.

---

## Long-Term Persistence

An attacker may establish a persistent presence before executing the final objective.

<pre>
Initial Compromise
        ↓
Establish Persistence
        ↓
Maintain C2
        ↓
Remain Undetected
        ↓
Execute Objective
</pre>

This allows the attacker to choose when to perform the final action rather than immediately revealing their presence.

---

## Countermeasures Against Actions on Objectives

Defenders should focus on **limiting what a compromised system or account can do**, detecting suspicious activity, and recovering quickly after an attack.

## 1. Data Loss Prevention (DLP)

**DLP** solutions help detect and prevent unauthorised transfer of sensitive information.

They can monitor data movement through channels such as:

* Email
* Web uploads
* Cloud storage
* External devices
* Network transfers

<pre>
Sensitive Data
      ↓
DLP Monitoring
      ↓
Detect Unauthorised Transfer
      ↓
Block / Alert
</pre>

## 2. Backup and Recovery

Reliable backups are essential for recovering from:

* Ransomware
* Data destruction
* System corruption
* Accidental deletion

A good backup strategy should include:

* Regular backups
* Tested restoration procedures
* Appropriate access controls
* Offline or otherwise isolated backup copies

The goal is to ensure that an attack does not permanently destroy critical data.

## 3. Network Segmentation

**Network segmentation** divides a network into separate security zones.

For example:

<pre>
User Network
     │
     ├──── Application Network
     │
     ├──── Database Network
     │
     └──── Critical Systems
</pre>

If one segment is compromised, segmentation can limit the attacker's ability to reach other systems.

This is particularly useful for reducing **lateral movement**.

## 4. Strict Access Controls

Access controls determine **who can access which systems and data**.

Organisations should apply the **Principle of Least Privilege (PoLP)**:

> Users and processes should receive only the permissions required to perform their tasks.

This reduces the potential impact of compromised accounts.

## 5. User Activity Monitoring

Monitoring user activity can help identify abnormal behaviour.

Examples of suspicious activity include:

* Unusual login times
* Unexpected access to sensitive files
* Large data transfers
* Unusual DNS activity
* Access to systems that a user normally does not use

The important factor is identifying behaviour that deviates from the organisation's normal baseline.

## 6. Endpoint Detection and Response (EDR)

**EDR** continuously monitors endpoints for suspicious activity and helps security teams investigate and respond to threats.

Examples of activities EDR can detect:

* Processes accessing sensitive files
* Unusual file modification
* Mass file encryption
* Suspicious process execution
* Unauthorised network connections
* Persistence mechanisms

<pre>
Endpoint Activity
       ↓
EDR Monitoring
       ↓
Detect Suspicious Behaviour
       ↓
Alert / Investigate
       ↓
    Respond
</pre>

---

## Breaking the Kill Chain

The complete Cyber Kill Chain can be represented as:

<pre>
Reconnaissance
      ↓
Weaponisation
      ↓
Delivery
      ↓
Exploitation
      ↓
Installation
      ↓
Command & Control
      ↓
Actions on Objectives
</pre>

The objective of defence is **not only to detect the final attack**. Security controls can break the chain at multiple stages.

<pre>
Recon       → Reduce exposed information
Weaponise   → Detect malicious payloads
Delivery    → Block phishing / malicious content
Exploit     → Patch vulnerabilities
Install     → Detect persistence
C2          → Detect malicious communication
Objectives  → Prevent theft, disruption, and abuse
</pre>

## Key Takeaways

* **Actions on Objectives** is the final stage of the Cyber Kill Chain.
* Attack objectives depend on the attacker's **motives and target**.
* Common objectives include **data exfiltration, ransomware, financial theft, destruction, disruption, espionage, and lateral movement**.
* Attackers may maintain **long-term persistence** before executing their final objective.
* **DLP** helps prevent unauthorised data exfiltration.
* **Backups and recovery** reduce the impact of ransomware and destructive attacks.
* **Network segmentation** limits lateral movement.
* **Least privilege and access controls** reduce the impact of compromised accounts.
* **User activity monitoring and EDR** help detect suspicious behaviour.
* The most effective defence is to **break the attack chain before the attacker reaches their objective**.
